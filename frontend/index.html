Four things a base site has to get right

Sunlight

Is there power?

For every hour we compute the Sun's altitude and compare it with the real terrain horizon in that direction, built from LRO LOLA elevation data. From that comes the visible part of the solar disc, the power reaching a panel that lies flat, stands upright or tracks the Sun, and the energy it collects over time.

Earth contact

Can we talk to home?

Earth hangs almost still in the lunar sky, but libration makes it wobble by several degrees. Near the poles it slips behind the hills, and direct radio contact ends. We track Earth's altitude against the same terrain horizon.

In Compare sites and the simulator's Surface tab

Resources

What is in the ground?

Lunar Prospector's gamma-ray spectrometer mapped the surface chemistry: FeO, TiO₂, Al₂O₃, CaO, MgO, SiO₂, K₂O and oxygen in weight percent, thorium and uranium in ppm. Choose a point and a radius and get the averages, a contour of the area and a CSV export.

In the simulator's Resources tab

Orbit

How does a spacecraft stay up there?

The Moon's gravity is lumpy. Mass concentrations under the maria (mascons) bend low orbits, and some decay into the ground. The Orbit tab propagates a spacecraft in that field, with Earth and Sun perturbations, and draws its ground track. Presets include LRO, Gateway's halo orbit, Luna 10 and PFS-2.

Each chart has its own vertical scale: at site A the whole sky story happens within a few degrees of the horizon. Hover or tap a chart to read the values, plotted every 2 hours. Download the data (JSON)

At the pole the Sun never sets in the mathematical sense. It circles 1–2° above the horizon all month. But at this spot the surrounding terrain stands higher for 70% of the time, and Earth drops behind it for two thirds of the month. A ridge a few kilometres away can see far more of both, so the terrain horizon is computed from elevation data for every site instead of being assumed flat.

Apollo 11's site has the opposite trade-off: two weeks of day, two weeks of night, and Earth always high in the sky.

Inside the simulator

One page, three tabs, one clock. Press Play or type a UTC time and every view follows.

Surface
Stand anywhere on the Moon and look around. The terrain is built in your browser with WebGL2 from LOLA elevation and albedo tiles, and the sky shows the Sun, Earth and the stars for the chosen time. A port of the team's geografia.py.
Orbit
Launch a spacecraft into the Moon's uneven gravity field, including mascons and Earth and Sun perturbations, and watch its orbit, ground track and diagnostics charts evolve. Physics runs on the server in batches and the browser plays them back smoothly. A port of neworb.py.
Resources
Pick a latitude, a longitude and a radius R. The map outlines the area and the charts show the average abundance of each element inside it, ready to export as CSV. A port of radius.py.

How it works

  1. Data

    Hourly Sun and Moon positions from JPL Horizons for 2000–2100, LRO LOLA elevation, global Moon mosaics and Lunar Prospector composition maps.

  2. Models

    The team's Python scripts do the science. The backend imports them as they are, so the physics on the website is the physics in the scripts.

  3. API

    A FastAPI backend wraps each script as a module with a typed contract. Heavy numerics stay on the server, with numba for orbit propagation.

  4. Browser

    The pages draw the results: WebGL2 for terrain and sky, canvas for maps and charts.

Data and conventions

Ephemerides
JPL Horizons, geocentric, ecliptic J2000. Hourly Sun and Moon vectors from 1 January 2000 to 1 January 2100, stored in metres in coords/<year>.csv.
Elevation
LRO LOLA gridded topography from NASA PDS, read at 64 pixels per degree with HTTP range requests. Falls back to 16 pixels per degree, then to USGS Astrogeology.
Composition
Lunar Prospector gamma-ray spectrometer maps from the NASA PDS Geosciences Node, registered to a 2:1 equirectangular base map.
Constants
Lunar radius 1,737.4 km, Earth radius 6,371 km, solar constant 1,361 W/m².
Time
UTC everywhere. The comparison page also accepts a local time zone, by country or UTC offset, and converts it.
Angles
Degrees. Azimuth is measured from north, clockwise (east is 90°). East longitude is positive.
